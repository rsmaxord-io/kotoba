Anime is a $25 billion business. The people who draw it are not.

Japan's animation industry set another record last year. The studios that actually produce the drawings invoiced about a tenth of that, and six in ten of them saw profits fall. This is how the money moves — and where it stops.

01

Two numbers that should not belong to the same industry

One measures everything anime touches. The other measures what animators are hired to make. The distance between them is the story.

The headline figure comes from the Association of Japanese Animations, which counts the whole commercial footprint of anime: theatrical tickets, streaming licences, Blu-ray, toys, games, events, character goods, overseas distribution. By that measure the industry reached ¥3.8407 trillion in 2024 — roughly US$25 billion — growing 14.8% in a year and more than tripling in fifteen.

Then there is the number that decides whether a studio can pay its staff. Teikoku Databank, which surveys the production companies themselves, put the domestic anime production market at about ¥340 billion in 2024. That is what gets invoiced for the work of making animation: scripts, layouts, key frames, in-betweens, colour, compositing, sound.

Roughly nine percent of the boom reaches the layer where the boom is manufactured. The rest is licensing and retail, and it is collected by the companies that own pieces of the intellectual property — broadcasters, publishers, music labels, trading houses, toy makers, streamers.

Why the definitions matter

These two figures are not competing estimates of the same thing. AJA's total is a broad market measure; Teikoku's is a narrow production-revenue measure. Reading them side by side is the point, not a contradiction. Both are reported figures, not projections.

The growth is not where the work is

In 2024, overseas revenue grew 26% while the domestic market grew 2.8%. Overseas sales now account for 56% of the total. Masahiko Hasegawa, lead author of the AJA report, described the shift plainly: the overseas market now far exceeds local revenue, and the gap will widen. He also noted that growth increasingly comes from bundled contracts spanning theatrical rights, streaming, merchandising and events — not simply from selling finished episodes.

Bundled deals are good news for rights holders. They are indifferent news for a subcontracting studio paid a fixed sum to deliver episodes on a schedule.

02

A record year in which most studios lost ground

Teikoku Databank's survey of 177 production companies found the profit squeeze concentrated exactly where the drawing happens.

The share reporting higher profits fell more than 13 points year on year.

The reason is not a lack of demand. It is a lack of hands. With more projects commissioned worldwide and a fixed pool of trained animators, studios are forced to extend schedules, which raises costs against budgets that were agreed earlier. Teikoku describes the result as a “profitless boom.”

The split inside the industry is sharp. Subcontracting studios — the ones that take overflow work and draw the frames — averaged ¥438 million in annual revenue, their highest since 2007. But only about 38% of specialised studios were in the black in the 2023 survey, with more than four in ten posting losses. Lead studios have IP revenue to absorb cost overruns. Subcontractors generally do not.

Scale protects the largest players and leaves everyone else exposed. The number of studios doing business with foreign companies rose to 45.2%, up 4.4 points in a year — a sign that international money is arriving, though not necessarily at better margins.

Growth today includes bundled contracts that span theatrical rights, streaming, merchandising, and events — not just content distribution. Masahiko Hasegawa, lead author, AJA Anime Industry Report

The Japanese government has made animation a pillar of its Cool Japan strategy, with targets for the content industry measured in trillions of yen by 2033. Policy attention has followed the money up the chain — to rights, exports and tourism. The bottleneck is further down, at the desk where a 24-year-old draws the frames between the key poses.

04

What would actually change the number

Not more demand. Demand is not the constraint.

Three levers show up repeatedly in industry reporting. The first is pre-funding from streamers, which can pay studios earlier and at higher rates than the committee model, though it also concentrates commissioning power in a handful of platforms.

The second is studio equity in the IP. When a studio sits on the committee rather than subcontracting to it, it shares in merchandising and licensing revenue instead of only invoicing for labour. A small number of studios have moved this way; most have not, because the investment required is capital they do not have.

The third is collective bargaining and transparency. The NAFCA survey — the first union-led survey of anime workers — found a median hourly rate of ¥1,111, slightly below Tokyo's minimum wage of ¥1,113, in a city where 85% of studios are located. Publishing that number is itself a form of pressure.

None of these fixes the shortage of trained animators, which is a decade-long problem. All of them change who absorbs the cost of it.
