Structure 02 · Labour

Paid by the drawing, not by the hour.

Japanese animation runs on piece rates. The entry-level job — drawing the frames between the key poses — paid an average of ¥2.63 million in 2023. The national average for all private-sector workers that year was ¥4.60 million.

01

Two jobs, one ladder

In-between animation is where almost everyone starts. It is also where the arithmetic is worst.

Animation is built in layers. A key animator (genga) draws the extreme poses that define a movement. An in-between animator (dōga) draws the frames that carry the motion from one pose to the next. Both are paid per drawing, per cut, at rates negotiated between the studio and the worker — with no hourly floor and, in most cases, no overtime premium.

The structure creates a predictable trap. New entrants take in-between work because it is the only door in. The per-drawing rate is low, so income depends entirely on speed. Speed comes from repetition. Repetition is how you get good enough to move up to key animation, where the rates are several times higher. The industry depends on this ladder, and the ladder depends on people being willing to be underpaid for two or three years.

Many are not. The labour shortage now constraining production is, in large part, a retention problem at the bottom of that ladder.

What changed since 2009

JAniCA's 2009 white paper put average in-between income at ¥1.1 million — roughly US$10,000 at the time — and caused a public shock. By 2023 the figure had more than doubled to ¥2.63 million. Conditions genuinely improved. They did not improve enough to close the gap with the national average.

02

Hours fell. They are still long.

JAniCA's surveys show a real, sustained decline in monthly hours since 2008 — and a 2021 average that is still far above the national norm.

A 27% reduction over thirteen years. 198 hours a month is still about 45 hours a week.

The trend line is the strongest argument that the industry can reform. Hours came down, and output did not collapse — the number of titles in production continued to rise. The improvement came from a mix of contractual pressure, studio policy and a tighter labour market giving workers more leverage.

But the union survey tells a harsher story about the present. NAFCA, a newer organisation that surveys workers directly rather than studios, found an average of 219 monthly hours across 323 respondents — about 34% above the national average of 163.5 hours. The median hourly rate derived from reported income and hours was ¥1,111, marginally below Tokyo's minimum wage of ¥1,113, in a city that hosts around 85% of the country's studios.

The two datasets are not contradictory. JAniCA measures a broad membership over time; NAFCA measures a smaller, self-selected group of respondents who may be younger or more precariously employed. Read together, they describe an industry where the average improved while the bottom of the distribution did not.

Piece rates make this harder to fix than a wage table would. There is no hourly clock to audit, no overtime line to challenge. A worker who takes three days to draw a cut that was budgeted at two has not worked overtime — they have simply earned less per hour. The cost of inexperience, or of a demanding cut, is borne by the person holding the pencil.

03

Why the shortage is the pressure point

Demand for anime is not the constraint on production. Trained hands are.

With more series commissioned than the workforce can service, studios compete for the same finite pool of experienced animators. That competition has started to move pay at the top: key animators, animation directors and specialists can now command rates that would have been unthinkable a decade ago, and some studios have begun advertising salaries rather than piece rates to attract them.

At the bottom, the effect is weaker. In-between work is the easiest to outsource, the easiest to deskill, and the first place a squeezed schedule cuts. The shortage does not automatically lift the entry-level rate; it lifts whatever is scarcest, and that is experience.

This is the mechanism that connects the two halves of this site. The committee keeps the production fee fixed. The studio absorbs schedule slippage. The animator absorbs the studio's margin problem through the piece rate. Each layer is behaving rationally. The result is a record-breaking industry in which the people who make the product are the least protected participants in it.

Definitions

Genga — key animation: the defining poses of a movement.

Dōga — in-between animation: the frames connecting key poses.

Cut — a single shot in the edit; piece rates are usually quoted per cut or per drawing.

A note on averages

Survey averages describe a distribution, not a person. Some animators earn well above these figures; some earn less than half of them. The median is often more revealing than the mean, which is why the NAFCA hourly figure matters.
