Structure 03 · Revenue

The boom is real. It is also somewhere else.

Anime's money now comes mostly from outside Japan, and mostly from rights rather than from making the show. That is a strong business model for the companies that own the property — and a weak one for the companies that build it.

01

Five streams, one of them narrow

Anime earns in more places than it used to. Almost all of the new places sit upstream of the drawing desk.

Historically, a late-night anime series was a loss leader. It ran on a broadcaster's schedule, sold discs to a few thousand dedicated buyers, and existed mainly to advertise the manga or the light novel. The committee model made sense in that world: the show was marketing, and the rights holders were the ones being marketed to.

Streaming broke that logic. A global platform will pay for exclusive or windowed rights in territories the original committee never expected to reach. That revenue arrives before and independent of disc sales, and it is denominated in the growth numbers now driving the headline market figure.

The five streams

Broadcast and advertising. Domestic television still provides the slot and part of the funding, but advertising revenue is flat and tied to a shrinking medium.

Streaming and licensing. The growth engine. Overseas revenue reached ¥2.17 trillion in 2024, 56% of the total, up 26% in a year. Deals increasingly bundle theatrical, streaming, merchandising and events into a single contract.

Theatrical. A hit film is the most profitable single asset in the business, and overseas theatrical has become a meaningful second window for major titles.

Packaged media. Once the core of the fan economy, now a collector's niche. Still high-margin per unit, but no longer the reason a show gets made.

Merchandise and events. Often the largest and least visible stream: figures, games, collaborations, pop-up cafés, exhibitions, stage events. This is where character IP compounds over years, and where the committee structure pays off most for rights holders.

The bundling effect

AJA's lead author attributes recent growth to bundled contracts rather than simple distribution deals. That matters for labour: a bundle raises the total value of the property without necessarily raising the production fee paid to the studio that makes it.

02

Why the studio does not see the growth

Revenue and margin are different things, and the industry's structure separates them cleanly.

The arithmetic of a squeeze

A fixed production fee, a schedule that slips, and a labour pool that cannot expand quickly. Every extra week of production is a cost the studio carries and the committee does not reimburse.

That is why a record revenue year and a profit decline can be true at the same time.

Teikoku Databank's survey of 177 studios found that 60% of lead contractors reported lower profits or outright losses in FY2024, and that the share reporting higher profits fell by more than 13 percentage points. The cause it identifies is not weak demand but production capacity: too many projects, too few animators, schedules extended beyond plan, costs rising against fees agreed in advance.

The subcontracting tier shows the same pattern from a different angle. Average revenue for specialised studios rose 14% to ¥438 million — a genuine record — while the share of those studios in the black stayed low, at roughly 38% in the 2023 survey. More money is moving through the system. It is not settling at the bottom.

There is a structural reason for that, and it is not incompetence. A studio paid per project cannot capture compounding IP value. If a series runs for five seasons and sells merchandise for a decade, the studio's income is five production fees. The committee's income is a decade of licensing. Both parties signed a contract that says so.

The overseas market now far exceeds local revenues, and this gap will only widen. Masahiko Hasegawa, AJA Anime Industry Report

Government policy has noticed the export number. Animation is a pillar of the Cool Japan strategy, with content-industry targets measured in trillions of yen by 2033. The risk of that framing is that it optimises for the metric — overseas sales — while leaving the production base that generates it structurally unchanged.

03

What would move the split

Three mechanisms, each with a cost.

Lever 01 Platform pre-funding

Streamers commissioning directly can pay earlier and above committee rates. It also concentrates commissioning power in a handful of buyers — a different dependency, not the absence of one.

Lever 02 Studio equity in IP

A seat on the committee converts labour income into rights income. It requires capital that thin-margin subcontractors do not have, which is why only a few studios have managed it.

Lever 03 Transparency and bargaining

Published rate data and union surveys put a number on the gap. NAFCA's finding of a median hourly rate below the Tokyo minimum is an example of evidence doing political work.
